UX Case Study · 2024

Response Tab, a functionality built to fulfil the critical and complex needs of apps like RFQ and Auction.

My Role
Senior UX Designer
Platform
Web (B2B procurement suite)
Methods
Competitive Analysis · Industry Frameworks · Product Walkthroughs
Outcome
New response functionality, shipped and adopted by enterprise customers

IENT is enterprise software. Companies use it to buy the materials and services they need to run their business. Every step of that process from raising a purchase requisition to paying an invoice is handled in its own app inside the platform.

Every app runs on the existing app structure, which holds five functionalities. Details carries the record's information. Attachments holds any files linked to it. Conversations is where the parties involved communicate. History logs every change made to the record. Workflow guides the approvals that move it forward.

The Requirement

Two new apps, with their own requirements

It was planned to introduce two new apps, Auction and RFQ, needed for our procurement software, and they came with their own set of requirements. It was expected that every requirement these apps needed had to be mapped onto the same existing app structure used by all apps, without extending it.

The Gap

What the existing app structure could not carry

From the initial research, we mapped the functionalities both apps needed, and almost all of it sat within the existing app structure, the routine every app already shares. One thing did not: the event itself, the live, competitive core that makes an Auction or an RFQ what it is.

The existing app structure · five functionalities
Details Event information, terms and conditions, line item information, expiry-extension rules
Attachments Files related to the event, contract templates, reference images
Conversations Questions and doubts from participants, requests from the buyer
Workflow The approval flow for the event, visible to the buyer
History A record of every modification and action in the event
Most of what Auction and RFQ needed mapped in here, as with any app.
Left outside · no home in the five
The event
Not a record or a form, but a live happening where vendors respond and the buyer decides. Nothing in the five functionalities could hold it.
What came next

The event was the one thing the structure had no place for. So the real question was what an event actually is, and what running one demands. Answering that, in enough depth to design for it, is where the research began.

Findings

What the event is made of

The research broke the event open. It runs through three stages, and what each role does shifts across them. In each, one behaviour matters most, and that is what the design had to put first.

Scheduled
Intent
Keep the vendor prepared before it starts.
Buyer
  • Can still adjust details or rules before it starts
Vendor
  • Studies the requirement, rules, and line items to preparematters most
Running
Intent
Keep the event competitive throughout.
Buyer
  • Watches the field move
  • Tracks who leads and by how muchmatters most

Buyer

Vendor
  • Sees where they stand

Three things stood out across the stages.

The running stage is where the event lives. It is the most crucial phase, holding the most activity and demanding constant attention from both roles. It is why the event exists in the first place.

Comparison is the thread that never stops. Vendors compare their position to decide whether to revise; the buyer compares the field to decide at all. It is the most constant behaviour in the event, running through every stage.

The award is the act that gives the event its purpose. It is the buyer's one decisive, irreversible decision, and it is what the whole event builds towards. Without it, nothing that came before means anything.

Direction

One place to hold all of it

The gap forced a choice

The event was the one thing the existing structure could not hold, and by now we understood it in full: how it moves through its stages, and what matters most in each. The question left was where it should live.

One place, not five

One option was to spread them across the functionalities the apps already had. That would break a single continuous event into five separate places and strain the template every other app relied on, so we ruled it out. The activities belonged together, in one functionality built to hold all three requirements at once.

The Response Tab

That functionality is the Response Tab. We built it as a tab so it sat inside the Auction and the RFQ alongside the existing ones, extending the structure instead of replacing it. Everything the event demanded now had one place to live, for both roles.

Solution

From research to shipped design

The research gave direction. What follows is how that direction became a working design: the journeys that scoped the tab's job, the lifecycle that shaped its behaviour, the four decisions that set its structure, and the shipped result.

The Base Design

What these decisions produced

This is the version of the Response Tab that shipped before any customer-specific configuration. The choices that shaped it are called out below.

Summary bar
Read by both roles. A buyer reads it for the bigger picture of how the RFQ is progressing; a vendor reads it for their own positioning and the rules that apply to them. The same panel, each reading what matters to them.
Two-column split
The left lists every vendor at a summary level (name, bid, rank) so the whole field is scannable at a glance. Selecting one opens its full response on the right. Overview on one side, depth on the other.
The detail worth noticing

Where a tool sits tells you what it affects

The tab carries two distinct sets of actions, and where each one sits is what tells you what it affects.

Outside the response panel
The comparative statement, vendor reminders. These act on the whole RFQ.
Inside the response panel
Past versions, and Award as the default decision. These act only on the selected vendor.

Placement encodes scope. A buyer never has to wonder whether an action applies to one vendor or all of them. Its position answers that before they click. And because the response panel was built as a defined zone rather than a fixed set of buttons, it could absorb new actions later without disturbing anything around it. The response content follows the same logic, grouped into sections by category so a buyer scans to the right part quickly and new fields slot in later without breaking the layout.

The Other Role

The same tab, seen by the vendor

A vendor opens the very same Response Tab, but their job here is the opposite of the buyer's. They do not compare a field of vendors; they submit one response and track where it stands. The skeleton holds; every region is turned toward that task.

The summary bar reads $7,500 (Rank 03) as the vendor's standing bid; the $6,200 in the form below is a new bid being entered, the edit-and-resubmit flow in use.

Same panel, their numbers
The summary bar is the same component the buyer sees, but permissions swap its contents. The vendor reads their own bid and rank, and the lead bid, rather than the whole field of responses.
The split, inverted
Where the buyer's left column listed vendors to choose between, the vendor's left is their own response form. The right holds the auction's line items, the requirement they are responding to.
The action slot, kept
Submit Response sits exactly where the buyer's Award did, in the response header. The same rule holds across both roles: the action on the response lives with the response.
Validation

Then Konica came

The design was built to adapt, but adaptability is only a claim until something tests it. Konica, one of the first enterprise customers to adopt the Response Tab, was that test. Their procurement was far more than a buyer picking a winner, and it reshaped what the tab had to do.

What the requirement changed
Approval before it's live
An RFQ no longer went straight out. It now passed through a multi-level approval chain before vendors ever saw it.
Responses can be returned
A vendor's response was no longer simply received. It could now be sent back (for amendment or rejection) rather than only accepted.
More than awarding
The decision was no longer a single award. Roles now selected a preferred vendor, voted, and approved, and some added their own input directly in the tab, rating each response against weighted criteria.

This is where the early decisions paid off. Because the action slot and information panels were already role-aware and permission-driven, Konica's actions slotted into the existing structure. The slot already shipped with Award as its default; selecting a preferred vendor, voting, and approving simply joined it. No structural redesign. Their process became a configuration, not a custom build.

How the decisions held

Each decision, tested by a real requirement

01 · Two-column layout
What Konica tested
More roles now reviewed the same responses.
How it held
The compare-left, detail-right frame carried all of them. No new layout needed for the extra reviewers.
02 · One shared, role-aware panel
What Konica tested
HOD, Steerco and MD each needed different things from the same response.
How it held
The panel served each by permission, and the rating proved it: a whole new scoring section appeared inside the detailed response, visible only to the HOD, without disturbing the panel for anyone else.
03 · The action slot
What Konica tested
Award was joined by Vote and Select Preferred Vendor.
How it held
These button actions dropped into the existing slot. Placement still signalled scope, no exceptions added.
04 · Structure to scale
What Konica tested
The requirement brought more fields, and an entire scoring section, into the response.
How it held
The grouped, sectioned structure absorbed them, including the rating grid as a new section, without the layout breaking.

One requirement proved the principle twice.

Konica's department leads needed to rate each vendor against weighted criteria, but a rating means nothing without seeing the weight behind each criterion. The weightage itself was set elsewhere in the product, not in the tab.

So the Response Tab pulled that weightage in and displayed it as read-only context (visible only to the department lead, only at the scoring stage) alongside the inputs to rate each vendor.

It was the same principle behind the shared key-info panel, proven a second time: one surface, showing different information to different roles based on permission and stage. Two independent instances of the same idea, which is what made it a principle, not a one-off.
